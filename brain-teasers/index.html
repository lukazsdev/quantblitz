10 brain teasers you will be asked in quant interviews

Ranked by how often they come up in trading and research interviews. The interviewer doesn't care whether you've seen the puzzle. They care whether you can explain the reasoning out loud. Try each one before you open the answer.

1The airplane seat

100 passengers board a full plane in order. The first has lost their boarding pass and sits in a random seat. Everyone after sits in their own seat if it's free, otherwise in a random free seat. What's the probability the last passenger gets their own seat?

Solution

1/2.

Whenever a displaced passenger picks at random, the only two seats that decide the outcome are seat 1 and seat 100: picking any other seat just passes the problem on. Those two seats are always equally likely to be chosen at that moment, so by the time passenger 100 boards, exactly one of them is free, with equal probability. It doesn't matter how many passengers there are.

225 horses

You have 25 horses and a track for 5. No stopwatch, you only see the order. What is the minimum number of races to find the fastest 3?

Solution

7 races.

Race five groups of five (5 races), then race the five winners (race 6). The winner of race 6 is the fastest overall. Only five horses can still be 2nd or 3rd: the 2nd and 3rd from race 6, the 2nd and 3rd from the overall winner's original group, and the 2nd from the group of the horse that came 2nd in race 6. Race those five (race 7): its top two are 2nd and 3rd overall. Six races can't be enough, because after six you can't have separated all of those candidates.

3Two eggs, 100 floors

There is a floor above which an egg breaks when dropped and below which it never does. You have two eggs. What's the fewest drops that guarantees you find the floor?

Solution

14.

Drop the first egg at floor n, then n + (n − 1), then n + (n − 1) + (n − 2), and so on, so that each time it breaks you have exactly enough drops left to scan the gap one floor at a time with the second egg. You need n + (n − 1) + … + 1 ≥ 100, so n(n + 1)/2 ≥ 100: n = 14 gives 105, n = 13 gives only 91. Drops at 14, 27, 39, 50, 60, 69, 77, 84, 90, 95, 99, 100.

4Burning ropes

Two ropes each take exactly 60 minutes to burn, but they burn unevenly, so half a rope doesn't take 30 minutes. Measure 45 minutes.

Solution

Light rope A at both ends and rope B at one end. When A is gone, light B's other end.

A lit at both ends burns out in exactly 30 minutes however uneven it is. At that moment B has 30 minutes of burning left; lighting its other end halves that to 15. Total: 45. The idea to say out loud: lighting both ends always halves the remaining time.

5Monty Hall

Three doors, one prize. You pick a door. The host, who knows where the prize is, opens another door showing nothing, and offers you a switch. Switch or stay?

Solution

Switch. It wins 2/3 of the time.

Your first pick is right with probability 1/3 and that never changes, because the host can always open an empty door whatever you picked. So the prize is behind the other unopened door with probability 2/3. The follow-up they like: with 100 doors and the host opening 98, switching wins 99/100.

6Flipping for two heads

Flip a fair coin until you get two heads in a row. What's the expected number of flips? And for a head followed by a tail?

Solution

6 for HH, 4 for HT.

Let E be the expected flips from scratch and F the expected flips once you hold one head. E = 1 + ½F + ½E, and F = 1 + ½·0 + ½E (a tail sends you back to the start). Solve: F = 1 + E/2 and E = 2 + F give E = 6. For HT, once you hold a head another head doesn't hurt you: F = 1 + ½·0 + ½F so F = 2, and E = 2 + F = 4. Progress towards HT is never undone, which is why it's faster.

7The die with a re-roll

You roll a die and get paid its face value. Before you're paid, you may re-roll once and take the new value instead. What's the game worth? And with two re-rolls?

Solution

4.25 with one re-roll, 4.67 with two.

A plain roll is worth 3.5. With one re-roll, keep anything above 3.5, so keep 4, 5, 6 and re-roll 1, 2, 3: (4 + 5 + 6)/6 + (3/6)(3.5) = 2.5 + 1.75 = 4.25. With two re-rolls, the first roll is worth 4.25 if you give it up, so now keep only 5 and 6: (5 + 6)/6 + (4/6)(4.25) = 1.833 + 2.833 = 4.67. Work backwards from the last roll. This is the whole idea behind pricing an option to re-decide.

8The bridge at night

Four people must cross a bridge at night with one torch. At most two cross at a time, and a pair moves at the slower one's speed. They take 1, 2, 5 and 10 minutes. Fastest total?

Solution

17 minutes.

1 and 2 cross (2), 1 returns (1), 5 and 10 cross together (10), 2 returns (2), 1 and 2 cross (2). The trick is sending the two slow ones together so the 10 is paid once and the 5 is never paid at all. Escorting each slow person with the fastest costs 19.

9Russian roulette

A six-chamber revolver holds two bullets in adjacent chambers. Your opponent spins, pulls the trigger and survives. It's your turn: spin again or pull straight away?

Solution

Don't spin: 1/4 risk instead of 1/3.

Spinning gives 2 bullets in 6 chambers: 1/3. Without spinning, you know the last chamber was one of the four empty ones, and only one of those four is followed by a bullet, so the risk is 1/4. If the bullets were not adjacent, two of the four empties would be followed by a bullet and it would be 1/2, so you'd spin. Conditional probability, dressed up.

10Ants on a stick

100 ants sit at random points on a 1-metre stick, each facing a random direction. They walk at 1 cm per second, and when two meet they both turn around. What's the longest it can take for all of them to fall off?

Solution

100 seconds.

Two identical ants bouncing off each other is indistinguishable from two ants walking straight through each other. So the stick clears at the time the furthest ant from its exit would take to walk off unhindered: at most 100 cm at 1 cm/s. The positions and directions don't matter.
